Start here

Point to one thing that changed because of something you posted.

A person who showed up, a dollar that moved, a vote, a policy, a door that opened. Take a minute. Most people come up empty, and that is the correct starting place for today rather than a failing.

The distinction

What is the difference between a message that needs an answer and a post that needs nothing from anybody?

Texting your landlord “the heat has been out three days, when is someone coming?” goes to one person who now has to respond or visibly refuse.

Posting “landlords in this town are a joke” goes to four hundred people, none of whom owe you anything. Both are true. Only one of them lands on somebody.

Here's the idea

Communicative capitalism: when your political speech stops being a message and becomes one more unit of circulation.

That is the concept, and the political theorist Jodi Dean gave it that label in 2005, before the iPhone. Her argument: on networked platforms, speech is valued for moving rather than for being received and answered. The count is the product. The passion that could have become action gets spent as posting instead, and it feels like doing something. That feeling is the trap.

Her phrase for the result is the foreclosure of politics: the loop closes, the energy is used up inside it, and nothing exits into the world.

What it looks like now

An AI-generated image, shared about 47 million times in roughly two days, in May 2024.

“All Eyes on Rafah.” Not a photograph. A rendered image of a vast tent camp with the words spelled out in the tents, spread through an Instagram Story template that made resharing a single tap. It became one of the most-shared images in the platform's history, including by large advocacy accounts.

Hold the politics of the conflict aside and look only at the mechanism, because that is what this class studies. The share was the entire act. It asked nothing of the person sharing, it reached nobody who had to answer, and it was engineered for frictionless circulation. Dean described the shape of this in 2005 and the platforms have only gotten better at it.

What runs out

Anyone can post forever, for free. Information is effectively unlimited. So what is actually scarce?

Here's the idea

“A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.”

Herbert Simon wrote that in 1971, and he was not writing about social media, which did not exist. He was an economist and cognitive scientist, later a Nobel laureate, and he was advising organizations on how to design their internal information systems now that computers could produce more reports than any human could read.

His insight generalizes: once information is abundant, the scarce resource is the attention it consumes. Anything scarce can be priced, bought, and sold. Fifty years later that is the entire business model of the platforms you use.

The consequence

The free app is not a charity. Your attention is the raw material.

So there are two different questions here and they get confused constantly.

The cause's question is how to capture attention. That is the one every campaign asks, and it is the easier one.

Your question, as the person being harvested, is where your attention goes and who decides. Not whether to pay attention, but what you are willing to spend it on and what you refuse. Those are different skills, and a campaign built by someone who has never asked the second question tends to produce exactly the kind of post that circulates and lands nowhere.

A way out that is not quitting

Jenny Odell wrote an entire book called How to Do Nothing, and she is not telling you to log off.

Her “doing nothing” is two moves. Refuse to hand your attention to the machine that converts it into circulation. Then re-place it on something specific: a place, a community, the actual people and living things in front of you.

For a cause, that is a design instruction rather than a mood. Aim the same energy at things that produce a reply and a relationship that outlasts the scroll. You cannot organize what you never actually noticed.

The case · who they were first

Eight years of grinding work came before the famous video. That order matters.

Invisible Children was founded in 2004 by three young filmmakers after a trip to Uganda, to campaign against Joseph Kony and the Lord's Resistance Army, which had abducted children as soldiers for two decades.

For eight years they did the slow version: campus screenings, student chapters, lobbying trips, letters. In 2010 that work helped produce the LRA Disarmament and Northern Uganda Recovery Act. In October 2011 President Obama deployed about 100 US military advisers to help regional forces pursue the LRA.

Note the date. That deployment came five months before the video. The political win was bought by eight years of organizing, which is the argument we spent all of last week on.

The case · the video

March 5, 2012. About 100 million views in six days, the fastest-spreading video in history to that point.

The strategy was stated openly: make Kony famous, so that the advisers already deployed would not be quietly withdrawn. By that narrow measure it worked. US support was sustained and later expanded, the State Department added Kony to its war crimes rewards program with a five million dollar bounty, and in 2014 additional special forces and aircraft were committed.

So it is not accurate to say the campaign did nothing. It held political attention on a conflict the news had abandoned, and that is an accomplishment worth stating.

The case · what it did not get

Kony was never captured. He is still at large.

The LRA was degraded to roughly a hundred fighters, and in 2017 the US and Uganda ended the pursuit without him. The campaign's own stated goal, the thing on every poster, did not happen.

And the backlash was immediate, from serious people, and some of it was earned:

Kony had left Uganda around 2006, so the film's picture of the conflict was years out of date. Critics called it a textbook white savior narrative, a phrase Teju Cole put into wide use that same month, in which African agency disappears and American teenagers become the protagonists. The organization's finances drew scrutiny over how little reached direct services. A public screening in Lira, in northern Uganda, reportedly drew anger from survivors who felt the film was about somebody else's catharsis.

The case · what they do now

They stopped making viral campaigns, and that was a decision, not a collapse.

Invisible Children still exists. The work looks nothing like 2012. They run locally-led peacebuilding and community protection in central Africa: early-warning radio networks that let remote villages warn each other, defection messaging aimed at fighters still in the bush, and programs run by people from the region rather than filmed for an American audience.

Read that as a strategic answer to the criticism. They concluded that the thing they were good at, capturing enormous attention, was not the thing that protected anybody, and they rebuilt around what did.

Who

He left Harvard in 1964 to register voters in Mississippi and did not come back to finish his degree for twenty-eight years.

Marshall Ganz spent sixteen of those years organizing farmworkers alongside Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta. He eventually returned, finished, and now teaches organizing at the Harvard Kennedy School. The structure he built is called public narrative.

The origin matters for this assignment. He developed it to train farmworkers, many with little formal schooling and no interest in being writers, to speak publicly and persuasively. It is a structure precisely because it had to be teachable to someone who had never done it. Which is the same reason their strategy document has to be something a volunteer could pick up and tell.

The structure

Three parts, in order, each doing a job the others cannot.

Story of Self

Why this claims me. One specific experience, first person, told as scene.Alone: a personal essay. Moving, and nobody knows what to do with it.

Story of Us

Why it claims the people I am talking to. The shared condition, handing the audience their own memory back.Alone: a press release. True, and nobody is in it.

Story of Now

Why it has to be answered this week. A deadline that exists on its own, and the choice facing the audience.Alone: a demand from a stranger.

They hand off. Self earns the right to say Us; Us earns the right to ask for Now.

Ask the room

Each of the three parts carries a challenge, a choice, and an outcome. One of those is missing from almost every first draft. Which one, and what does its absence do to the story?

The answer

The choice is the one that goes missing, and without it they have written a report.

A challenge and an outcome with nothing between them is just sequence: this happened, then that happened. The choice is the only place values become visible, because a choice shows what someone was willing to do when they did not have to do anything.

The test to give them: can a reader point at the sentence where somebody decided something? If not, the part is unfinished, however well written.

The case · the model they can read

Watch how small a usable choice is.

The challenge

“In tenth grade, I got my period during second period. I knew what had happened before I even stood up. I checked my backpack. Nothing. I asked the girl beside me. She didn't have anything either.”

The choice

“A few weeks later, I asked if I could help keep that drawer stocked. I brought in a box of pads. It was a small decision, but it changed what I noticed about school.”

From “Ready from Bell to Bell,” the completed example now linked on the Major Projects page. The challenge is one morning. The choice is a box of pads. The outcome is not a victory but a shift in attention: she starts asking why a predictable need depends on knowing which teacher has a drawer. Nobody needs a dramatic life to write this. They need one moment where they decided something.

Section 6

How the story travels is not a posting schedule. Every piece has a job.

Piece one

Introduces the frame. A thirty-second reel: pencils in a cup, paper towels, soap, an empty shelf, then that shelf stocked.Instagram, because the frame is visual.

Piece two

Explains the opportunity in plain language. The new state law, and the board's official contact information.Facebook, because that is where the parents and community groups are.

Piece three

Supplies the deadline and the action. “One email. Five minutes. Friday.”Plus direct messages to PTA members and teachers, which turn agreement into a relationship.

Platforms get assigned by what they actually do, not by what the student already uses. Sequence matters only because the jobs are different. The test to hand them: point at any piece in your campaign and say what job it does that no other piece does. Two pieces with the same job means you have one piece and a duplicate. A piece with no job is there because posting felt like progress, which is what this entire class period has been about.
Section 7

Two words they use interchangeably, and the rubric does not.

Outcome

The change you want in the world, stated so somebody could tell whether it arrived.

The district publishes a supply-access and restocking plan.

Indicator

Evidence you could observe that tells you whether the outcome is getting closer.

Thirty-five confirmed emails by October 23.

The outcome is the point. The indicator is how you find out early whether you are getting there.

Ask the room

A campaign reel reaches 41,000 accounts and brings in 1,200 new followers. Outcome, indicator, or neither?

The answer

Neither, and they already know why. They spent the first half of today on it.

Reach measures circulation. If circulation can rise while nothing in the world changes, which is Dean's claim exactly, then a number made of circulation cannot be evidence that something changed. Report it if you like. It cannot be what the strategy rests on.

So the indicators have to be made of something else. The model uses three kinds, and the top rubric band asks for all three.

The three kinds

Language, behavior, relationships.

Language

Somebody else starts using your words. The model codes how the issue gets described beforehand (an emergency, a donation, a student problem, an expected part of school operations), then looks for the readiness frame in comparable public texts afterward.Dated screenshots, so you are not remembering the language you hoped to hear.

Behavior

Somebody does something that costs them a little. Thirty-five confirmed emails by a date; three people willing to speak at public comment.Showing up is a much higher bar than agreeing, which is why this one resists faking.

Relationships

People who did not know each other are connected without you in the middle. Four people who met through the campaign now text each other directly.This is the one that predicts whether anything survives after they graduate.

An indicator with no method behind it is a hope. Collect the “before” first. The top band asks for a stated way to collect each one. Tell them today, because a baseline they do not capture this week cannot be captured later: gather the before-texts now, keep dated screenshots, count confirmed actions rather than claimed ones, and let people self-report voluntarily.

Workshop

Take one piece of pure circulation and wire it to action.

Each group picks one move, a viral hashtagan online petitiona boosted videoa black-square solidarity posta shared infographic and fills all four boxes. Pick someone to report back.

What it circulates

What does this move spread well, and what reach can it realistically get?

e.g. a viral hashtag spreads a feeling and the sense that many people agree. It can reach millions of strangers and almost nobody with authority over the thing.

Where it dead-ends

Who has to receive it or answer it right now? If the answer is nobody, that is the dead end.

e.g. a petition with 40,000 signatures delivered to a company under no obligation to read it. Nothing compels a response, so no response is the correct prediction.

Who could answer

Point to one actual person or office with the power to say yes. Where should this land?

e.g. not “the university” but the Director of Dining Services, who holds the contract. Not “the town” but the five people on the planning board who vote in November.

Wire it to action

The one ask, channel, or captured contact that converts reach into a reply.

e.g. the infographic now ends with one line, “Email these five board members before Friday,” the addresses sit in the caption, and a sign-up link turns the people who cared into a list you can reach again.

Where we go from here

Stop feeding the flow. Start building the thing.

Before "how do I get more reach," ask "if this circulates perfectly, does anything change, and who has to answer for it?"
