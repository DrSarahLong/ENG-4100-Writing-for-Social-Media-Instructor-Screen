Debate this

“Forty thousand people saw it. That means it worked.”

You have spent a semester on this one. Make the argument anyway, as strongly as you can, before we answer it.

Here's the idea

Æffect: one word, because you have to report two things.

The Center for Artistic Activism jammed affect and effect together because artists and organizers kept measuring different halves and talking past each other.

Affect is what it moved in people: what they felt, said, remembered, or understood differently. Effect is what it changed in the world: a policy, a practice, a budget line, a decision, a count.

Effect

The district published its supply-access plan eleven days after the meeting, and the page says who restocks it.

Affect

“I didn't know you were allowed to just take one.” Said at the shelf at 11:15 on the 28th, written down that day, used with permission.

The first one goes in a table. The second one is why anybody came back the next week, and it will never fit in a table.

The effect half, laid out

Five rungs, and vanity lives on the bottom two.

5 · Power. A decision changed. Access to somebody you could not reach before.
4 · Relationship. Six names and numbers in a list you own, who will act again.
3 · Action. 164 of 200 cards came back with somebody's handwriting on them.
2 · Engagement. Likes, comments, shares on the photograph of your artifact.
1 · Reach. Forty thousand views of that photograph.

Rungs 1 and 2 are not worthless. They are fuel, and fuel is only worth something if it drives the engine. The test on any number: does it move something to rung 3 or higher?

Call it out

Vanity, impact, or depends? Three fast ones.

You made sixty buttons and all sixty were gone from the table by noon.
Eleven people told you at the table that they loved the project.
Six people asked how they could help with the next one.

Say which, and say why. Argue with each other before I say anything.

Here's the idea

All three are “depends,” and what they depend on is a decision you still get to make.

The buttons. Free things leave a table. You measured supply, not interest, and you do not know whether one of them was ever worn. Count how many you see on backpacks the following week and you have a behavior instead.

The eleven compliments. Praise at a table is the analog version of a like: cheap, pleasant, and offered to your face by people who do not want to be rude. It becomes evidence if you wrote down what they actually said and when.

The six volunteers. Six people who asked and walked away are six people you cannot find. Six names and numbers is rung 4. The difference between those two outcomes is a clipboard.

The rule

Decide what counts as success before the artifact goes out.

A metric chosen afterward will always be the one you happened to hit. A metric chosen in advance can tell you no, and that is the only reason to have one. Write the number down with a date beside it.

Worked example. “Two hundred cards out. Target one hundred and twenty back. Under sixty would mean the prompt failed, not that nobody cares.” Three sentences, written before anything ran, and the third one is what makes the first two useful.

Six ways to measure with no money

You cannot run a survey. Build the counting into the artifact instead.

The counts you have

Put out a known number, count what is left. 200 out, 164 back, 36 unaccounted for. Say the 36 out loud.

A comment wall

The artifact collects the data. Code the 164 cards for whether they say “donate” or “should already be there.” That is your language indicator, measured.

A speak-out, with a scribe

One person's only job is writing down what gets said, with the time. That is how affect becomes quotable.

Call and response

Two jars and a bowl of beans. “Did you know the state has a program for this?” 38 yes, 121 no. A baseline for two days' work.

An interactive prop

A board with 240 blank squares, one per apartment. At the end, 83 are filled. The photograph of it is the finding.

The two-week follow-up

Go back to the same place and ask five people whether they remember seeing anything. The cheapest useful thing on this list.

The observation sheet Copenhagen used is already built for you: Unleashing Creative Campaigns, PDF page 65, linked on the Project 3 details page. Hand it to somebody who agrees to watch your action instead of taking part in it.

Where that list comes from

A three-day experiment on a bridge in Copenhagen, 2018.

The Center for Artistic Activism, with Silas Harrebye of Roskilde University and Stephen Duncombe of New York University, ran conventional tactics against creative ones on the same issue, same bridge, same hours, for three days. A speech against a performance. A petition table against something stranger.

Their instruments were a clipboard and a notebook: 108 spot interviews with passersby, 30 structured observation sheets filled in by people watching rather than taking part, video of both conditions, and a follow-up survey two weeks later to test what stuck.

The creative versions beat the conventional ones on nearly every quantitative measure. And the words people used split cleanly: annoying, lecturing, predictable on one side, funny, surprising, captivating on the other. One bridge, one city, three days, so it is a strong result and not a law. Every instrument they used is something you could run on Sanford Mall this week.

What a good evaluation paragraph sounds like

From the report on a campaign against a 40% rent increase.

“Nine people spoke, not the eleven who had signed up. 140 of 200 zines went. The chalk was gone in one rain and almost nobody saw it, which was a design failure we should have predicted. The paper ran the story and used the word ‘increase.’ Affect: four people told us it was the first time they had been in a room with their neighbors, and one cried, which no metric caught.”

Count the moves in that paragraph. What is it doing that most campaign write-ups refuse to do?

Here's the idea

Five moves, and four of them are about the parts that went wrong.

A shortfall stated as a number rather than as a feeling. Nine, not eleven.
A count with a denominator. 140 of 200, so a reader can judge it.
A failure diagnosed instead of apologized for. Chalk was the wrong material for the goal.
One piece of effect evidence from outside the campaign. The paper used their word.
One piece of affect evidence the writer explicitly says the numbers missed.
Workshop · the rest of the period

Draft your evaluation paragraph in that shape.

Use whatever you have so far. If the artifact has not run, write it in future tense with the numbers you are committing to today.
Include the thing that went wrong. If nothing has gone wrong yet, write the thing most likely to, because something will.
Bring it to me and read it out loud. A paragraph that sounds defensive when you say it will read that way too.
