Where that comes from

Two reasons, and neither of them is talent.

Your first answers were everybody's answers. Round one produced the same handful around the room: mark a hazard, save a parking space, use it as a megaphone. Those arrive first because they are the uses you have already seen, and what you have already seen is, by definition, what nobody notices.

Then a constraint did the work that inspiration gets credit for. “It cannot be a cone” blocked the default and pushed you somewhere you would not have gone on purpose.

You just ran a version of Alternative Uses, built by the psychologist J.P. Guilford, who in his 1950 address to the American Psychological Association argued that the field had almost entirely ignored creativity, and who then spent years building the first tests for it. He gave us the term divergent thinking: producing many possible answers instead of converging on one correct one.

And in 1957 Christensen, Guilford, and Wilson reported something in these tasks that has kept replicating since. The later responses in a person's list are reliably the more original ones. Idea one is a cliché. Idea twelve is the one nobody else in the room wrote. The only thing separating them is that you kept going.

So: persistence, plus a constraint that blocks your default. That is the method you are about to use on your own campaign.

Here's the idea

Ask any room for a campaign idea and you get the same five.

A poster. An Instagram page. A tabling event in the student union. A canned food drive. A flyer on every door in the building.

Those five are not bad ideas. They are the available ideas, which is a different thing. They arrive first because they are what you have already seen, and the fact that you have already seen them is also why nobody notices them now.

Same phenomenon as the traffic cone, on the thing you actually have to build. So we are going to do the same two moves to your campaign: keep going past the obvious ones, and add a constraint that blocks the default.

The constraint

At least one of your artifacts cannot be distributable on social media.

The artifact

Something a person receives by being somewhere, holding something, or doing something alongside other people. A zine handed over. Chalk marks down a sidewalk showing how far it actually is to the nearest grocery store. A booth with two chairs in it. A stack of cards carried into a room.

Documentation

The photograph of that, which you will take and which you will need. Photograph everything, post what you want to post, and bring the object itself to your presentation if it is something you can carry. The artifact and the record of it are two different deliverables.

You can still use social channels. You just have to defend the choice instead of defaulting to it.

The method

Ten tactics in ten minutes. The first seven have to be impossible.

This comes from the Center for Artistic Activism, founded in 2009 by Stephen Duncombe, a scholar of political culture, and Steve Lambert, an artist, who have run this exercise with thousands of organizers. Their instruction, word for word, is quantity over quality.

An impossible tactic is one you have no money for, nobody to help you do, and not enough time to pull off. It can break the laws of physics, it can cost a billion dollars, and it can require ten thousand sandwiches or a singing dragon.

Still a tactic

“A choir follows the dean to every meeting for a month.” Somebody does something. Impossible, and an action.

Not a tactic

“Everyone suddenly cares.” Nobody does anything. It is not impossible to get people to care, but more importantly, it is not an action.

Workshop · ten minutes on the clock

Write ten. Seven impossible, then three possible.

One idea per minute. Keep writing even when it is bad, especially when it is bad.
Numbers one through seven are impossible. If one of them seems achievable, it is not impossible enough.
Numbers eight through ten are possible, and you write them last.
No crossing out. You are not editing today, you are generating.
Share

Here's the idea

An impossible idea usually has a possible one hiding inside it.

Start from this impossible one: every unlit crosswalk on campus gets its own lighthouse.

Scale it up

Twice the budget, every day for a month. A lighthouse at every crossing in the county, burning all night. Pushed that far it breaks, and what is left standing is the thing underneath it: the danger is invisible until somebody lights it.

Scale it down

The smallest version that still does the thing. One person at that crosswalk at dusk with a camping lantern, every Tuesday, for three weeks.

Steal the mechanism

Throw out the lighthouse and keep how it worked. The mechanism was making an invisible danger visible after dark, which a roll of reflective tape and forty chalk outlines of near-misses also does, for nine dollars.

Worked example

One impossible idea, run through all three moves.

Impossible: a forty-foot sculpture on Sanford Mall made entirely of menstrual products.

Scale up: one in front of every school in the county. Still impossible, and it tells you the idea is about scale.

Scale down: two hundred small cards, each with one person's handwritten line, hung at eye height across a walkway so you have to walk through them.

Steal the mechanism: the spectacle was never the point. The point was size assembled out of individual pieces, which is the quilt move from Week 8. That mechanism costs about fourteen dollars and three weeks.

The fit test

Four questions, and a yes to all four means start building.

Could you physically have the first one of these finished within two weeks, with the money and the hands you actually have?
Does it need a body in a place? If somebody could get all of it from a screenshot, it does not count for the constraint.
Does it carry your Project 2 frame, the same conflict and the same ask, without a caption explaining that it does?
Can you count something about it? How many taken, how many returned, how many stayed, how many filled in.
