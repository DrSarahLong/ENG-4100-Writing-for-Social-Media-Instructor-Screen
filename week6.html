Context

A copy of what, exactly?

In the Soviet Union, the State owned the printing presses, the paper supply, the copy machines, and the licenses. Nothing reached print without passing a censor.

So "banned book" did not mean hard to find. It meant it did not legally exist.

What people risked prison to copy

Solzhenitsyn on the labor camps. Pasternak's Doctor Zhivago, published abroad in 1957 and banned at home. Akhmatova's Requiem, about the Terror. Orwell's 1984. Bulgakov's The Master and Margarita. Trial transcripts. Bibles. Lists of who had been arrested.

Why any of that was illegal

Each one contradicted the Party's official account of its own history: that there were no camps, no famine, no Terror, no dissent worth naming. A censor's job was not to protect readers from lies. It was to protect one story from competitors.

Copying or passing these texts fell under the anti‑Soviet agitation statute, Article 70 of the Russian criminal code after 1960. Up to seven years in a labor camp, plus five years' exile. Not for writing it. For retyping it, or for having a copy in a drawer.
Here's what they did

Samizdat

Russian: sam (self) + izdat (publishing), so "self-published." The underground copying and hand-to-hand passing of banned texts, roughly the 1950s through the 1980s. The word is a joke at the State's expense: Gosizdat was the State Publishing House.

Carbon copy

Stack thin paper with inky carbon sheets between, hit the keys hard enough to bruise the platen, and one typing makes up to six copies at once. The sixth is nearly unreadable. The whole "press" fits on a kitchen table, and the cc: line in your email is named after it.

One typing plus carbon paper makes about six copies at a strike. 6 readers → each retypes → 36 → 216 … No headquarters to raid. The distribution system lives in people's kitchens and coat pockets. The reader is the publisher.
The text we're about to copy

Sixteen lines cost him his life. Here they are.

Osip Mandelstam, a Russian poet, wrote "The Stalin Epigram" in November 1933. He never published it. He recited it aloud to about a dozen people. Someone told.

He was arrested in 1934, exiled, arrested again in 1938, and died that December in a transit camp near Vladivostok.

“The Stalin Epigram” · Osip Mandelstam, November 1933
Our lives no longer feel ground under them. At ten paces you can't hear our words. But whenever there's a snatch of talk it turns to the Kremlin mountaineer, the ten thick worms his fingers, his words like measures of weight, the huge laughing cockroaches on his top lip, the glitter of his boot-rims. Ringed with a scum of chicken-necked bosses he toys with the tributes of half-men. One whistles, another meows, a third snivels. He pokes out his finger and he alone goes boom. He forges decrees in a line like horseshoes, One for the groin, one the forehead, temple, eye. He rolls the executions on his tongue like berries. He wishes he could hug them like big friends from home.

Translated by W. S. Merwin and Clarence Brown, from Against Forgetting, ed. Carolyn Forché (W. W. Norton, 1989). Read it free at poets.org/poem/stalin-epigram

What it was resisting

By 1933 Stalin was not a politician you criticized. He was the subject of official poetry, painting, and song, addressed as a father and a genius. Meanwhile the countryside was starving.

Mandelstam's poem does two forbidden things. It describes the silence everyone was living inside, and then it breaks that silence by describing the man himself in a human body, with fingers "fat as grubs." No reverence. No title. Just a mountaineer from the Caucasus with thick fingers.

It was never printed in the USSR in his lifetime, and no copy in his own hand survives. His widow Nadezhda memorized his poems so they could not be seized. When every medium can be confiscated, the last storage device left is a person. The poem survived in a human body for thirty years.
Exercise · the only copy is in your head

Two minutes. Learn this well enough to say it.

Samizdat carried poems, but mostly it carried facts: who was taken, when, and where to go. Here is the same job, now. A message that has to move, and that nobody wants written down.

Fourteen families at Ridgeview got eviction notices on Tuesday. The notices give them thirty days. Three of the fourteen are on the tenant council. The council meets Thursday, 7 p.m., Sixth Street library, back room. Bring anyone who got a notice. Do not put this on Facebook.

No writing. No photos. No notes. In two minutes this comes off the screen, and the only place it exists in this room is inside the people in it. (Made-up building, real situation.)

Then it moves. We'll run three separate chains at once, from three different starting people, so keep track of which chain you're in. Recite it quietly to one person only, never to the room. You get one pass, with no repeating, no going back, and no corrections from anyone listening in. If you've lost a piece of it, do what anyone would do: fill the gap and keep going. Last person in each chain, hold what you have, because you'll say it out loud.

What the exercise was for

Memory is the one medium nobody can search, seize, or subpoena. It is also the least reliable one you will ever use. Three things you just watched happen:

The body is the last medium

Nothing to confiscate. Nadezhda Mandelstam held her husband's poems this way for roughly thirty years, through his arrests and his death, because paper could be taken and she could not be read.

Accuracy is the first casualty

Numbers slide, days move, addresses blur. The Chronicle's entire value was being exactly right about names and dates, which is the hardest thing for this network to carry.

The safety line goes first

Check whether "do not put this on Facebook" made it to the end of all three chains. The instruction protecting everyone is usually the shortest, the least interesting, and the first thing dropped.

If a message can only travel through people who will stand close and listen, what does that predict about who actually carries risky work?
Name it · the media theory for this week

It predicts the people closest to you. Strong ties carry risk; weak ties carry information.

Doug McAdam, a sociologist studying the 1964 Mississippi Freedom Summer, asked in a 1986 study why some students went south to register Black voters and others, who believed exactly the same things, stayed home. Belief did not predict it. Relationships did. The volunteers who went were the ones with strong personal ties to people already going.

His term for work that can get you hurt is high‑risk activism, and his finding is that it travels through strong ties almost exclusively. You will hand a dangerous poem to someone you would take a punch for. You will not hand it to 4,000 followers.

Why this matters for your project. In 2010 Malcolm Gladwell used McAdam's finding to argue that social media builds mostly weak ties, and therefore cannot produce real movements. Zeynep Tufekci, whom you'll be reading, disputes that.
The shape of the tool

A printing press is central, costly, and seizable. A typewriter with carbon paper is none of those. The tool's limits, slow and by hand and six copies at a time, forced the network to be decentralized. There was no center to raid and no subscriber list to demand. The weakness was the security feature.

Now run the same test on the tools you actually use. Every platform has a shape, and the shape decides things for you before you write a word. Where does your cause's network physically live, and who can seize it? A follower list lives on a company's server and can be sold, leaked, subpoenaed, or switched off. A group chat lives on 30 phones. A printed flyer on a laundromat corkboard lives in a room with no login. An Instagram account with 12,000 followers is one policy change from zero. Pick a tool and you have picked a politics, whether or not you meant to.
The turn

Before anyone is ever arrested, what does being watched do to people?

Samizdat existed because people were watched. You will not face the KGB, but your cause still lives under watchful systems: platforms that log everything, employers who search names, landlords, opponents who screenshot.

Name it · the chilling effect
In June 2013 it became public that the NSA was collecting bulk internet data. Jonathon Penney, a legal scholar, went looking for what that did to ordinary behavior, and published the results in the Berkeley Technology Law Journal in 2016. Traffic to Wikipedia articles on terms like "car bomb" and "Al Qaeda" fell measurably after that month, and stayed down. Nobody was arrested for reading them. People stopped on their own. That gap, between what is actually punished and what people stop doing anyway, is the chilling effect. For a movement the danger is specific: the members with the most to lose go quiet first, and they go quiet before you ever ask them to speak.
Applied · back to the bench

Who can safely post about hostile architecture, and who can't?

In June 2014 a man walking to work past 118 Southwark Bridge Road in London photographed a bed of metal studs set into the pavement outside a luxury building, and posted it. The photo spread, a hashtag formed, a petition passed 11,000 signatures, the Mayor of London called the studs ugly, and Tesco, whose subsidiary owned the site, agreed to pull a similar installation at its Regent Street store. Now ask who took that photograph. A commuter. Not a person sleeping in that doorway. The people the spikes were built to remove are the ones who cannot safely post: a photograph places them somewhere at a time, identifiable, on a platform their shelter, their caseworker, their family, or the police can search. The most affected person has the most to lose by appearing. That is the chilling effect operating inside a campaign that is otherwise going well, and it is why a campaign can succeed while the people it is for stay invisible in it.

Why is a network with no center harder to shut down than one big press?

Think about what an opponent would have to physically take in each case.

A case · Moscow, 1968 to 1983
The Chronicle of Current Events was a samizdat newsletter that did one dangerous thing: it documented Soviet arrests, trials, and abuses, dryly and accurately, for about fifteen years. Editors were arrested. The Chronicle kept coming. Its secret was structural. There was no center to cut. No subscriber list to seize, no single office to raid. Each issue told readers, in effect, "if you know of an arrest, pass the facts back up the same chain you got this from." Information traveled through trust, in both directions. The medium had no middle.
Applied · where does your list live?

The Chronicle had no list. You probably do.

One shared Google Sheet

Every volunteer's name, phone, and address in one link. Convenient, searchable, and one screenshot away from your opposition, a disgruntled ex-member, or a subpoena.

A public Facebook group

Membership is the list. Anyone who joins can scroll it. For a tenants' union, that means a landlord can learn exactly which of their tenants are organizing.

A phone tree, or small signal groups

Slower, and nobody can see the whole shape of it. Each person knows five people. No single capture gives you the network.

A follower count

Feels like reach, and it is, but you do not own it. Change the algorithm or the terms of service and your 12,000 becomes 400 with no notice and no appeal.
