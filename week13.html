Debate this

“A rough handmade thing beats a slick printed one every time.”

Take a side and defend it with something specific you have actually seen.

Here's the idea

Visible handwork carries information. Carelessness carries information too.

A hand-stitched object tells the person holding it roughly how many hours went into it, and that is data a printed card cannot transmit. Sarah Corbett, who founded the Craftivist Collective in London in 2009 after years in professional campaigning, built a practice on this: for a living-wage campaign aimed at Marks & Spencer, volunteers hand-embroidered a bespoke handkerchief for each board member. You cannot delete a handkerchief, and you can see the hours in it.

But the same channel runs the other way. An object that looks like you made it in the hallway five minutes before class tells the viewer how much you cared, and they will believe it. The line is not polish. It is evident time.

The five build decisions

An idea becomes an artifact when these five have answers you could hand to somebody else.

What it is, in materials. Not “a zine” but the paper, the size, the fold, the ink, the typeface and its size.
How many. A number, chosen for a reason, that you can actually produce.
Who makes it. You, or the participants? This changes what the object means.
Where it lives. An actual location, with permission if permission is needed, and a reason for that spot and not another.
How long it lasts, and where it ends up. Hours, weeks, permanent, and what happens to it afterward.
Worked example

The same five, answered.

What: two hundred 4×6 cards, 110lb cream stock, one hand-carved rubber stamp in black, one line written by the person in their own pen.

How many: two hundred, because the building has 240 units and two hundred was what the stock and three weeks of tabling could carry.

Who makes it: the stamp is mine, the line on each card is theirs. That is what makes the stack evidence instead of decoration.

Where: ground-floor atrium, step-free from two entrances, where people already stop and stand still.

How long: three weeks of collecting, then the whole stack carried by hand into the board's public-comment meeting and held up while one person speaks.

Debate this

“It's a physical object, so accessibility doesn't apply.”

Somebody argue for it, because it is the answer most people give, and we are about to take it apart.

Here's the idea

Seven questions a handmade artifact has to answer.

A website has standards, tooling, and a checker. A table in an atrium has a step, a font size, a noise level, and a forty-minute time commitment, and nothing is going to flag any of it for you. Project 3 asks you to handle at least three of these.

Physical access. Step-free route, no gravel, no curb, and can somebody seated reach the thing?
Print and sight. 14pt or larger, high contrast, no text over a busy photograph, a large-print version available.
Language. Plain language, and translation where your audience needs it, checked by somebody who speaks it.
Time, seating, and stamina. A chair, and a two-minute way to take part as well as the long way.
Sensory load. No sudden noise, no strobing, a quieter slot, a warning when part of it is loud.
Touch and non-visual access. Can a blind participant take part at all?
A route in for people who cannot be there. Presence is the point, so say how somebody who could not come still took part.
Worked example

Six answered as changes to the object, and one left open.

Physical: the shelf's writing surface drops to 30 inches so somebody seated can reach it, and the supplies go in the middle bin rather than the top one.

Print: everything at 14pt or larger, black on cream, twenty of the two hundred cards printed at 18pt.

Touch: the stamp is carved deep enough to emboss, so the mark can be felt, and the bin labels are raised paint.

Time: taking a supply is ten seconds, writing a card is two minutes, and there are two chairs.

Sensory: no sound element at all. A quiet object in a loud building, on purpose.

Away: a drop box at the front desk and a mailing address on the back of every card. Nine came in that way.

Language, not solved: forty cards were printed in Spanish, nobody who speaks Spanish checked the translation, and four came back. That goes in the report as a design failure, with the reason.

Workshop · fifteen minutes

Run the seven on your artifact. Write three as changes to the object.

Not “I considered accessibility.” Write what moves: the table, the type size, the location, the chair, the time slot.
Then write the one you are knowingly not solving, and the reason. That paragraph scores better than seven checkmarks, because seven checkmarks are rarely true.
Before you make two hundred

Make one, and hand it to five people.

Not a sketch. One finished unit, with the lettering on it, in the place it will live. Here is what comes back from five people and nothing else, on a one-sheet zine handed out at a bus stop.

Two of the five open it upside down, because the fold order was obvious to you and to nobody else. One cannot read 10-point type in the shade. One asks what they are supposed to do with it, which means your ask is on panel seven and belongs on panel two. One puts it in a pocket, which tells you the thing people want is smaller than the thing you made.

Twenty minutes, five people, and you redesign before you print instead of after.

The Center for Artistic Activism calls this prototyping, and their version is on PDF page 62 of Unleashing Creative Campaigns, linked on the Project 3 details page.

Debate this

A campaign chalked the old rent and the new rent down the sidewalk outside the leasing office. What killed it?

The imagery was good, it cost nothing, and it was difficult to argue with. Guess.

Here's the idea

It rained that night.

By morning there was nothing, and almost nobody had seen it. Their own report says: “The chalk was gone in one rain and almost nobody saw it, which was a design failure we should have predicted.”

And the fix: “Chalk was wrong for October. Next time the same information goes on a laminated card zip-tied to the mailbox cluster, where it stays dry and people stand still for thirty seconds anyway.”

This is not an argument against chalk. Chalk is excellent for a one-hour action you will photograph. They matched a material that lasts four hours to a goal that needed three weeks. Durability is a design decision, and it belongs in your five answers.

Workshop · the rest of the period

Three dates, and what could destroy it.

Date one: the first finished unit. Date two: full production done. Date three: the artifact in the world, with enough runway to measure it before you present.
Then write what could physically destroy, remove, or quietly nullify your artifact. Rain, facilities, a locked door, somebody taking it, nobody noticing it.
And what you are doing about that. Permission in writing, a photograph the day it goes up, a version that re-hangs in five minutes.
