Early 1980s · a new plague

If the people in power will not even say the name of the thing killing you, what does that silence tell you?

A new disease was spreading through gay men, drug users, and other people the country was already happy to ignore. The President gave no major public speech about it until 1987, years in, after tens of thousands of Americans were already dead.

Here's the idea

The silence was not sitting there unexplained. It already had a story attached, and the story was privacy. This was a private illness, in private lives, and decent people did not discuss it. Grief happened behind closed doors. Obituaries said "after a long illness."

So a movement had two jobs, not one. Break the silence, and take away the excuse the silence was hiding behind.

Look at it

Two words and an equals sign. What does that equation accuse somebody of?

The triangle is borrowed. In the Nazi camps, gay men were forced to wear a downward-pointing pink triangle to mark them for death. These designers turned it to point up.

Here's the idea
The frame they inheritedA private illness. Quiet deaths. Silence as discretion, which is to say silence as good manners.
The frame they builtSilence is not discretion. Silence is the mechanism. Somebody is choosing it, and the choosing is what kills.
Why that equation does so much work
  • it assigns an actor. Someone is being silent, which means someone could stop
  • it refuses the excuse. Privacy stops being kindness and becomes complicity
  • it makes you finish the sentence, so the conclusion is yours and you can't argue with yourself
  • flipping the triangle upward turns a mark imposed on you into a claim you make
The flipped triangle is a frame move dressed as a design move. The picture did not change much. Who was holding it changed completely.

Design activism

Making your argument with an image, a color, or a symbol instead of a paragraph. The picture does the arguing.

Gran Fury

ACT UP's design cell, formed 1988, named for the Plymouth the NYPD drove, a car whose name also means rage. They dressed protest in the visual language of advertising so it traveled the same rails everyone already looked at.

The scaling problem

Small groups trust each other but can't reach anyone. Big crowds have reach, but strangers don't trust each other or move fast together.

Affinity group

Five to fifteen people who know and trust each other, plan their own piece of an action, and look after their own members. Many groups plug into one big demonstration.

Why is a crowd built from small bonded cells more difficult to stop than one giant leaderless mob?

Drama on purpose

They lay down and played dead while the cameras rolled. Why stage your own death for a photograph?

Fake tombstones, blood-spattered lab coats, hundreds of bodies still on the pavement. All of it built for one viewer.

Here's the idea
Drama as strategy
  • a zap is fast, surprise, one target, embarrass them today
  • an action is big, planned, staged for the camera
  • the die-in hands the news a picture it can't resist, and the picture argues the equation without printing it
  • the bonded cells make the crowd difficult to behead: no single leader to arrest, trust lives in the group
FDA action, October 1988. Roughly 1,500 activists, about 180 arrests, on camera, on purpose. Within months the FDA changed its rules to release experimental drugs faster. The image did what a press release never could.
The AIDS Memorial Quilt (1987 on)
  • each panel is 3 by 6 feet, the size of a grave
  • one panel per person lost, sewn by the people who loved them
  • first laid on the National Mall in October 1987: 1,920 panels
  • now the largest piece of community folk art on earth
Quilting is one of the oldest folk media there is, and it runs deep here in Appalachia: memorial quilts, and the quilting bee, where a community gathers to sew and to grieve together.
Two speeds, one silence

The die-in and the Quilt fought the same silence. What did the slow Quilt do that a fast zap never could, and what did the zap do that the Quilt couldn't?

What this is called

Before any of it got designed, somebody decided what three things a stranger had to walk away with.

Those are key messages, and the look that carries them is style and tone. Both come from the Story Canvas, an eleven-box planning tool published by Digital Storytellers under a Creative Commons license, built on Osterwalder's business model canvas. Two of those boxes are Project 2 deliverables.

Key message 1
This is an epidemic, it is happening now, and the numbers are enormous.
Key message 2
Your government knows and is choosing not to act.
Key message 3
That choice is killing people, which means a different choice is available today.
Style and tone
The look of advertising. Black field, one saturated color, sans-serif capitals, no logo, no caption, no explanation. It borrowed the visual grammar of the glossy ad campaigns running beside it, so a poster about dying moved on the same rails as a poster about jeans.

Three messages, and the poster prints none of them. So why write them down at all?

Here's the idea

Because key messages are not copy. They are the test you hold the copy up against. Nobody ever printed "your government is choosing not to act," but every zap, every die-in, every poster had to deliver it, and anything that didn't got cut. Settle the three first and the design decisions stop being arguments about taste.

Style and tone does the same job for the look. "The look of advertising" is a decision you can hand to four different people and get four consistent objects back. "Make it powerful" is not.

Project 2 asks you for both. Three key messages for your own cause, sized so a stranger could repeat them, and a style and tone note with two reference images so somebody else could design to it. Thursday's module walks you through drafting them with the pink triangle as the worked example.

Workshop

Engineer one attention action the camera can't walk away from.

Each group takes one silence, a campus issue nobody talks abouta local closure or cuta workplace people won't talk aboutan environmental harm nearby and fills all four boxes. Pick a spokesperson to report back.

Worked example · wage theft at a regional warehouse The silence. Nobody is lying about it. It simply has a story attached already, which is that this is a payroll error and a matter between a worker and HR. Private. Individual. Boring. The single image. Forty time cards pinned to a clothesline strung across the sidewalk outside the main gate at shift change, each one with the unpaid minutes circled in red. One photograph, and you can count. The three key messages. It is happening to forty people, not one. The company has the records. Fixing it is a decision somebody could make Friday. The line not crossed. No worker's name or face appears without them asking for it, because the people in the photograph are the ones who can be fired.
The silence

Who is staying quiet, what story is the quiet hiding behind, and what does it cost the people you're fighting for?

The single image

Design one legal, doable action. What is the one picture the camera carries away, and what do you leave out so the viewer finishes the thought?

Your three key messages

Three things a stranger walks away able to repeat. None of them has to appear in the image.

The line

What would you not do to get attention? Say where the drama goes too far, and who bears the risk.
